Leadership

Officially Yes, Personally No

The hardest calls arrive when the payoff is uncertain and the room is against you. You make them anyway, and you own the result whichever way it lands. Most of what I know about leadership lives inside that sentence.

The decisions I'm proudest of were made with my back to the wall — a restructuring at Energy Labs, bets on ventures where I had no operational experience to lean on. We relabel those moments afterward as "good judgment," but in the moment they're just bets you're willing to be held accountable for. When the numbers come in green, everyone has a flattering word for you. When a call goes wrong, the people who cheered are suddenly hard to find.

I learned that with a product sitting on the launch pad, one button-press from live. Not a single board member backed it but me — they were nervous enough that they kept their distance from the company entirely, even after every commitment had been made to the right people. I went ahead with the handful who were willing to move. That product became an outsized success.

Conviction is cheap once you already know you're right. It only counts when you're standing alone, before the results are in.

But success breeds complacency. The easy support that arrived after the win quietly seeded the failure of the next product. When that one went sideways, nobody stepped up to share the call. The lesson wasn't that people are unreliable — it was that alignment built on momentum instead of belief is brittle. We had a real chance to build a durable advantage, and we lost it to the assumption that the last win guaranteed the next.

Here's what I actually took from it, stripped of the bravado I'd have used to describe it back then.

Separate the decision from the sentiment. Loyalty, guilt, the feeling that you owe someone for showing up early — all real, and all things that belong in how you treat people. None of them gets a vote on whether a strategy is sound. You can honour a relationship without letting it cast a business vote; they're different ledgers, and confusing the two is how good companies make bad decisions.

Commit visibly, and own the result either way. "Officially yes, personally no" isn't about concealing your doubts. It's about backing the call fully once it's made, instead of hedging so you can disown it later if it fails. The leaders worth following are the ones who say "this is the decision, and it's mine," and then carry the consequences in public.

Judgment is earned, not assigned. Anyone can critique a hard call from the cheap seats, with nothing at stake. The opinions I weight on a difficult decision come from people who have had to make difficult decisions themselves. The rest is commentary.

None of this is an argument for leading cold. The best teams I've been part of ran on trust, not fear — and warmth and clarity were never in tension. You can care deeply about the people you work with and still make the unpopular call, still hold the line on what the work requires. That's the job: make the call, own it completely, and let the results speak.
