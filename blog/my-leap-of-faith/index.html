My Leap of Faith

Sometimes a single phone call redirects your whole life. The person on the other end might be the last name on your list — or not on it at all.

On 14 August 2013 I took a call that changed everything. It was the boost I'd been waiting for, a kind of luck I didn't see coming — and it came from someone who wasn't even on my list. I'd never spoken to him, knew nothing beyond his name, didn't even have his number. So my first words were, "Who's this?" That was Ahaan Pandit. He has since become one of the few people I trust without conditions — an equal partner through the good stretches and the bad, the one who started it all with an idea and then found people, backed them, nurtured them, and stuck around. He taught me what faith and trust actually mean.

What came after that call was my leap of faith.

A leap of faith is the moment logic hands off to belief — the point where you want something badly enough that caution stops being useful and you trust the rest to conviction. Those leaps are risky by definition. But there isn't much life in a safe, tidy one. I was standing at a junction where a lot of pieces had to fall into place, with no experience to lean on and a group of near-strangers around me. To move at all, I had to beat my own instincts and start believing in other people — something I was genuinely bad at. Too cautious, maybe. Too afraid to fail, too fixated on getting it right the first time. None of that is wrong on its own, but worst-case thinking fills your head with enough noise that delegating anything that matters becomes almost impossible.

With little to lose and everything to gain, I took the risk. It took time, but I learned to trust my team. Some days disappointed me; some overjoyed me — that hasn't changed. What kept me moving was a belief I refused to put down: that I couldn't live a cautious, ordinary life, that I could build something better than what already existed, that I could make a real difference somewhere. The leap turned out to be exhilarating. Work turned into friendship. I gained a lot and lost some, and the team stayed — one stubborn pack of people who simply refuse to quit. And it was never only my leap. It was theirs too: a leap to trust a lone wolf to lead the pack.

A leap of faith matters because it lets you experiment with yourself, which is the most basic right any of us has. And experimenting matters because it keeps you alive. The day you stop experimenting with your own life is the day you begin, quietly, to disappear.

So when do you take it? When nothing new is happening — when your life starts to resemble standing water. When you want the pieces to fall into place badly enough to act, knowing full well that nothing is guaranteed. Life gives you the shot; landing it is your job. Deploy the parachute at the right moment, and you'll find there was more ground beneath you than you thought.

What did the leap cost, and what did it return? It cost us our laziness, our excuses, our procrastination — and a few relationships along the way. It returned one hell of a journey. Was it worth it? Every bit.
